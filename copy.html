01

The angle

The angle

Not the bot-farm story. The traffic was real, the installs were real — only the credit was fake. Someone was billing for users who were already installing.

This is the whole reason the post works. Most ad-fraud content shows warehouses of phones. This case explicitly ruled out device farms and emulators, which makes the reveal genuinely counterintuitive for a marketing audience and makes each reader do arithmetic on their own budget.

Everything in the design serves that reveal. Slide 3 is the slide that carries it; if only one slide gets extra attention, it is that one.

Audience: client-side UA and growth managers, brand and performance marketers, agency leads. Assume smart, sceptical, scrolling on a phone, allergic to agency self-congratulation.

Colour system — binding

Colour carries information in this set. It is not decoration.

Grey · #C7C1BC · normal / unproven supply Red · #D93B21 · contaminated Green · #2FA24A · proven / genuine Orange · #F58220 · Go Mobile intervention Ink · #14100E · type Paper · #FBF7F4 · background
  • Nothing outside this palette. No blue, no magenta, no rainbow stat cards.
  • Orange does not appear until slide 5. Orange means we act, and slide 5 is where we act. Slides 1–4 are the problem; they stay grey, red and ink.
  • A reader should be able to learn this language by slide 3 and then read slides 4–9 without the body copy.

The previous version of this carousel used five colours on one slide with no meaning attached to any of them. That is the main thing being corrected here.

04

Copy rules

  • Maximum ~20 words per slide. Word counts are given on each slide below. If a slide needs a paragraph, the device is wrong — flag it rather than shrinking type.
  • No caveats, disclaimers or methodology notes on any slide. All of it lives in the caption (section 06).
  • Sentence case in headlines. Full stops stay — they set the flat, declarative tone the case needs.
  • Numbers are labels, not headlines, except on slides 7 and 9.

06

Post caption

The first two lines are what show before “…see more” on mobile. They carry the whole hook.

No bots. No device farms.

The users were real. The installs were real. 22.2% of them just weren't caused by the ad the client was paying for.

↑ everything above the dashed line shows before “…see more”

We audited 13,000+ installs across 21 delivery days on one paid channel. Volume was on plan. Cost per install sat inside target. Nothing in standard reporting flagged a problem — because the network aggregates hundreds of publishers behind a single source name, and the average blended contaminated supply with clean supply into one acceptable-looking number.

Underneath that average, 10 placements out of 60+ were manufacturing click evidence to claim credit for installs that were happening anyway.

Two things made it survive three weeks:

→ Contamination arrived on day two, twice. A placement would open quietly, clear a first-day review, then take most of the daily budget the next day at double the rate. Weekly review misses the day the money goes.

→ Blocking didn't end it. The same supply returned under fresh identifiers inside 24 hours. A blocklist is a record of what already cost you money, not a defence.

So we stopped blocking what fails and started buying only what passes: a separate campaign restricted to placements that cleared every test across multiple days at real volume, with daily placement-level checks and new supply capped until it proves itself.

Fraud rate: 22.2% → 0.96%, then 14 consecutive clean days.
Cost per install: −31%. Cost per target in-app action: −47%.
Genuine installs per day: 525 → 588.

That last number matters most. Total delivery came down slightly, but real installs went up 12% — because the volume we removed was never real. Same network. Same contract. No pause in delivery.

What we did not claim: organic was not being cannibalised (daily organic held inside a 1.7% band throughout), and there were no device farms or emulators — hardware, network and location signatures matched genuine users across the whole buy. The problem was attribution, not fabricated devices. Post-restructure supply does show engagement patterns worth a direct conversation with the network about inventory type; we reported that as an open question rather than a finding.

A claim that overreaches gets dismissed along with everything attached to it.

Figures come from the client's own measurement platform, audited by us at raw event level. Engagement anonymised. Detection methodology, test thresholds and monitoring rules are proprietary and not reproduced here.

The same audit runs on any account with raw attribution data, usually inside a week. If your reporting is weekly and your supply is aggregated, it is worth knowing what is underneath the average.

Alternative openings

If the recommended opener feels too blunt for the page's usual voice:

  1. Your weekly UA report is five days behind a problem that turns in 24 hours.Leads on speed instead of the reveal. Safer, less distinctive.
  2. We removed 96% of the attribution fraud from a live channel without changing network, renegotiating the contract, or pausing delivery.Leads on the result. Strongest for existing clients, weakest for cold reach.
  3. One in five installs on this channel was already going to happen. Someone was billing for them anyway.Closest in spirit to the recommended version, slightly more pointed.
